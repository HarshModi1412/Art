The opportunity

The money is already in your shop. It leaves quietly.

Not because you are doing anything wrong, but because one person cannot watch everything at once. A brand with eight people watches all of it. Here is what goes out the door while nobody is looking, and what closing it is worth.

The customers who just stop coming

They do not complain. They do not cancel. They simply do not come back, and you find out months later. Getting one of them back costs a message; finding a new one costs an ad.

If 40 past buyers who spent ₹3,000 each are sitting uncontacted, that is ₹1.2 lakh of goodwill you already paid for.

The bestseller that was out of stock

Every day your best item is not on the shelf is a sale that goes to somebody else, and a customer who learns you run out.

One popular item, out for six days a month, at ₹900 a sale and two sales a day: ₹10,800 a month, gone without a trace.

The week you did not post

The brands growing on Instagram are not more talented. They are more regular. Four posts a week, every week, is the whole trick, and it is the first thing to go when you are packing orders.

Posting is the cheapest customer you will ever get. Skipping it costs nothing today and everything in a year.

None of this is a knowledge problem. Every big brand knows these three things. They have people to act on them. That is the only difference, and it is the difference this closes, for one person, in the time it takes to have chai.

What you get

The eight people you cannot afford, in one screen

Each of these is a job somebody does at a bigger company. None of them needs you to learn anything.

The one who reads the numbers

What sold, what it earned you, which days quietly lose money, and what next month looks like, from the sales file you already have. Plain sentences, not a wall of charts.

The one who watches your customers

Who buys often, who spends most, and who has gone quiet. The message to bring the quiet ones back is written for you, and we count how many actually returned.

The one who orders stock

It knows how fast each thing sells and how long your supplier takes, so it tells you to buy again before you run out, and hands you the order form for that supplier, filled in.

The one who shoots your photos

Upload five pictures you are proud of. It reads your light, your colours, your framing, and then every picture it makes for you looks like your brand, using your own product photo as the starting point so the item is really yours.

The one who posts every day

A whole week of Instagram planned as one story rather than seven unrelated posts: the tease, the reveal, the proof, the close. Captions written, times chosen, pictures made. You approve, it schedules.

The one who does the paperwork

Proper tax invoices with the right rates, and one file your accountant can file from. Nobody re-types anything.

The new part is the last three. Reading numbers has been possible for years. A tool that looks at your own photographs, works out what your brand looks like, and then shoots and writes a month of posts in that style: that has only been possible for about a year, and almost nobody has put it in front of a one-person business yet.

Built for Shopify's customer. Built to Odoo's standard.

Two kinds of software already serve small sellers, and each gets half of it right.

Shopify

Right customer. A person who sells things, set up in an afternoon, no consultant.

Thin where it counts. The storefront is the product; the operations are apps. Inventory is an app. Win-back is an app. Reports are an app. Each is ₹800–2,400 a month, none of them talk to each other, and none of them know your GST position.

Odoo

Right depth. Everything is joined up. Buying, stock, billing and tax are one system, so a delivery moves your stock and a sale sorts its own tax without you typing it twice.

Wrong customer. Built for a business with someone employed to set it up. A jewellery seller with 40 designs will not get through the setup, and the screens assume you were trained on them.

One Tap Manager

Odoo's joined-up insides. Products, stock, suppliers, orders, bills and GST are one thing, not six. An order on your website takes the item out of stock, uses up the materials it needed, writes a proper tax invoice and shows up in your numbers, without you entering anything twice.

Shopify's front door. Sign up, upload a CSV or connect a channel, and the first screen tells you what to do this morning rather than asking you to set up an accounting system first.

The difference in one sentence: Shopify tells you what happened and sells you an app to work out what to do. We tell you what to do, and the doing is already wired to the same data.
What we are not, yet. Odoo handles factories, several warehouses, salaries and full accounting. We handle none of those and are not pretending otherwise. If you run a factory floor and three godowns, buy Odoo. If you sell 40 to 400 products and your accountant just wants one clean tax file at the end of the month, that gap costs you nothing.
